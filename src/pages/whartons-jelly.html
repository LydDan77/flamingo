The Source

What is Wharton's Jelly, exactly?

Wharton's Jelly is the gelatinous connective tissue that surrounds the blood vessels inside the umbilical cord. It was first described by English physician Thomas Wharton in 1656. In the early 2000s, researchers found that it holds a large number of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and began to study it for regenerative medicine.

These MSCs are the same type of cell found in bone marrow and fat, with one difference: they come from newborn tissue, not from an adult. In the lab, cells from newborn tissue divide more quickly than cells from adult donors. That is a laboratory finding. It does not show that they work better in a person.

The umbilical cord is normally discarded after birth. With the mother's consent, it can be donated after a healthy, full-term delivery instead, with no harm to mother or child. Treatments made from it are not FDA-approved, and are investigational for most uses. Results vary.

FAQ

Wharton's Jelly questions answered

What is Wharton's Jelly and why is it used in stem cell therapy?{{icon:plus}}
Wharton's Jelly is the soft connective tissue inside the umbilical cord. It holds a large number of mesenchymal stem cells. Clinics use it because the tissue is donated after birth, so nothing is taken from the patient, and because the cells come from newborn tissue. Treatments made from it are not FDA-approved, and are investigational for most uses.
How is it different from bone marrow or fat-derived stem cells?{{icon:plus}}
Bone marrow and fat cells are taken from your own body, in a separate harvest procedure. Wharton's Jelly cells come from donated umbilical cord tissue, so there is no harvest, and the cells come from newborn tissue. Whether one source works better than another in people has not been settled.
Is Wharton's Jelly therapy safe?{{icon:plus}}
Every patient is screened by a licensed clinician first. Side effects are possible with any procedure, and we discuss them with you before you decide. All products we use come from accredited tissue banks and are tested for sterility, viability, and potency before use. These treatments are not FDA-approved, and are investigational for most uses. Results vary.
Which conditions might a clinician consider it for?{{icon:plus}}
Our clinicians consider it for joint and soft-tissue problems: worn joints (osteoarthritis), and tendon, ligament and other soft-tissue injuries. A clinician decides whether you are a candidate after reviewing your history. We do not address systemic diseases or conditions outside our clinical scope.
How is it administered?{{icon:plus}}
Therapy is delivered via targeted injection to the affected area by a licensed nurse practitioner in your home. No surgery, no clinic visit, no general anesthesia. A visit takes about an hour.
