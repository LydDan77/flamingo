Stem cell therapy or surgery? Seven questions to ask first

A surgeon has given you a date. Someone has mentioned a non-surgical option. Here is how to think it through without pressure from either side.

Nobody should talk you out of a surgery you need. Nobody should talk you into one you can postpone, either. The seven questions below are the ones our clinicians ask on a consultation, and they work just as well in a surgeon's office.

1. What exactly is damaged, and how badly?

Ask to see your imaging with someone who can explain it. The difference between wear, a partial tear and a complete rupture changes everything that follows. Regenerative options are generally considered for degenerative change and partial soft-tissue injury, not for a joint that has lost its structure or a tendon that has fully separated.

2. What happens if I wait three months?

Some conditions get worse with delay. Many do not. If a surgeon tells you the procedure is elective and timing is up to you, that window is where a non-surgical option can be tried first, with surgery still available afterward.

3. What does the evidence say for my condition?

Be specific. Evidence for regenerative therapy varies widely by joint and by diagnosis, and much of it is early. A reputable clinic will tell you where the research is thin. Stem cell and exosome products are not FDA-approved for orthopedic conditions and are considered investigational for most musculoskeletal uses.

4. Am I actually a candidate?

Age, activity, overall health, medications and the state of the joint all matter. Our own candidacy rules send a meaningful share of people back to their specialist with a clearer picture, and we would rather do that than treat someone who is unlikely to benefit. Who is, and isn't, a candidate goes into the detail.

5. What will it cost, in writing?

Surgery has a price that insurance may cover. Regenerative therapy is almost always self-pay. Ask for the full cost of a protocol before you decide, including follow-up visits, and ask whether financing is available. If a clinic will not put the number in writing, that tells you something.

6. Who is delivering the care, and who is overseeing it?

Ask for names. Ask about licensing. Ask who you call if something feels wrong on a Sunday. In our model a licensed nurse practitioner delivers the protocol in your home and a named physician oversees it, and both are written into your plan.

7. What does "success" mean here?

For surgery, success is usually defined by the procedure. For regenerative therapy, it should be defined by you: less pain on the stairs, a longer walk, sleeping through the night. Agree on that before treatment, and expect the honest answer that individual results vary and some people see little change.

If you take one thing from this page: get both opinions, ask for both in writing, and choose with the full picture. A good surgeon and a good regenerative clinician will each respect the other's role.
