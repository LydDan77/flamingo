Exosomes, the signals cells send each other

Cells do not work alone. They send each other signals, and exosomes are one way they do it: very small packages that carry messages from one cell to the next. Exosome products are not FDA-approved, and are investigational for most uses. Results vary.

The Science

What are exosomes, and why do they matter?

For years, researchers thought stem cells helped by turning into new tissue. Many now think the cells act mainly through the signals they send to the cells around them. This is still being studied.

Exosomes are one way those signals travel. They are tiny packages, 30 to 150 nanometers across, that cells release. Each one carries proteins, fats and genetic material. When it reaches another cell, it can change how that cell behaves.

In lab work and early studies, exosomes from cord-tissue cells have been seen to influence inflammation and repair in nearby cells. Whether that becomes a change you can feel depends on the condition, the product and the person. Published results vary.

Mechanism

Four things to know about exosomes

{{icon:radio}}

Messages between cells

They carry proteins, fats and genetic material from one cell to another. In the lab, this can change how the receiving cell behaves.

{{icon:layers}}

Made by cells

One cell releases many exosomes. The ones used in treatment come from cells grown from donated, screened umbilical cord tissue.

{{icon:target}}

Given by injection

A licensed nurse practitioner injects them into the affected joint or tissue, in your home.

{{icon:trending-up}}

Used alongside the cells

We may use them in the same visit as Wharton's Jelly cells. Whether the two together do more than either alone is not yet known.

FAQ

Exosome therapy questions answered

What are exosomes and how do they work?{{icon:plus}}
Exosomes are very small packages that cells release, including the cells in Wharton's Jelly. They carry proteins, fats and genetic material from one cell to another. Researchers study them for their part in inflammation and repair.
Are exosomes the same as stem cells?{{icon:plus}}
No. Stem cells are living cells that can divide. Exosomes are not cells. They are tiny packages that carry the signals those cells produce.
Which conditions might a clinician consider exosomes for?{{icon:plus}}
We use exosomes alongside cell treatment, for the same joint and soft-tissue problems: worn joints, and tendon or ligament injuries. A clinician decides whether they belong in your plan. Exosome therapy is not a standalone therapy for systemic diseases.
How are exosomes administered?{{icon:plus}}
Exosomes are typically administered via targeted injection to the affected area, often in the same visit as Wharton's Jelly cells, by a licensed nurse practitioner in your home.
Is exosome therapy FDA-approved?{{icon:plus}}
No. Exosome therapy is not FDA-approved, and is investigational for most uses. We give it only as part of a plan supervised by a physician, using products from accredited tissue banks. Results vary.
