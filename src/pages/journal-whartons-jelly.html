What Wharton's Jelly is, and why clinics use it

You will hear the phrase in almost every regenerative consultation. Here is what it means, where it comes from, and the questions worth asking about it.

A tissue, not a drug

Wharton's Jelly is the gelatinous connective tissue inside the umbilical cord. It cushions the cord's blood vessels during pregnancy. After a healthy, full-term birth, the cord is normally discarded. With the mother's informed consent, it can instead be donated to an accredited tissue bank.

Within that tissue are mesenchymal stromal cells, often called MSCs, along with growth factors and the structural proteins that hold the cord together. Because the tissue comes from a newborn, the cells have not been exposed to decades of aging, medication or injury. That is the main reason clinics are interested in it.

What the cells are studied for

MSCs are studied for the signals they send rather than for turning into new tissue. In laboratory and early clinical research they have been observed to release molecules that may influence inflammation and the local repair processes around a joint or soft-tissue injury. Research is ongoing, and results in published studies vary by condition, product and protocol.

The honest summary: Wharton's Jelly products are investigational for most musculoskeletal uses. They are not FDA-approved to treat any orthopedic condition, and no reputable clinic should tell you otherwise.

Sourcing and testing, and why they matter

Two questions separate a serious provider from a careless one. Where does the tissue come from, and what is done to it before it reaches a patient?

  • Accredited tissue bank. The cord should be donated, screened and processed by a tissue bank that is registered with the FDA and accredited by the American Association of Tissue Banks (AATB).
  • Donor screening. Each donor is screened for infectious disease and medical history before the tissue is accepted.
  • Lot testing. Every lot should be tested for sterility and endotoxin before release, with a certificate of analysis available on request.
  • Cold chain. The product is stored and transported frozen, and thawed only immediately before use.

How it is used in practice

In a musculoskeletal protocol, the product is delivered by injection into the affected joint or soft tissue by a licensed nurse practitioner under physician oversight. The visit itself is short. What takes time is the work before it: a consultation, a review of your imaging and history, and an honest conversation about whether you are a candidate at all.

What it is not

It is not a cure, and it is not a replacement for a surgery you genuinely need. It will not "regrow" a joint. Individual responses vary, and some patients do not notice a meaningful change. A clinic that promises a specific outcome is making a claim the evidence does not support.
