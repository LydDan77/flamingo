The word gets used loosely. This is the version a careful clinician would give you across the kitchen table.

Tiny packages that cells send to each other

Cells do not work alone. They coordinate, and one of the ways they do it is by releasing very small vesicles, far smaller than a cell, that carry proteins and genetic material to neighbouring cells. Those vesicles are exosomes. Think of them as sealed envelopes with instructions inside.

Exosomes are not cells. They cannot divide, they cannot become new tissue, and they do not stay in the body for long. What they may do, according to laboratory and early clinical research, is influence how nearby cells behave, including processes involved in inflammation and tissue repair.

Why "signaling" is the honest word

Marketing language often promises that exosomes "repair" or "regenerate". The research supports a narrower claim: exosomes carry signals that may support the body's own repair processes. Whether and how much that translates into a change you can feel depends on the condition, the product and the person, and published results vary.

Exosome products are not FDA-approved for any orthopedic or wellness use and are considered investigational. The FDA has issued consumer alerts about clinics marketing them with claims that outrun the evidence. We agree with the alerts.

Where they come from

The exosomes used in regenerative protocols are typically derived from laboratory-cultured mesenchymal stromal cells sourced from donated, screened umbilical cord tissue. As with any biologic, the questions that matter are the same: an accredited source, documented screening, lot testing for sterility, and a certificate of analysis your clinician can show you.

How a protocol works

Exosomes are offered on their own or alongside a stem cell protocol, typically in the same visit. They are delivered by injection to the affected area by a licensed nurse practitioner under physician oversight, after a consultation that decides whether the approach is appropriate for you at all.

What to expect

Mild soreness at the site for a day or two is common. Any change in symptoms, if it comes, tends to be gradual over weeks rather than immediate. Your care team schedules follow-up check-ins to track how you are doing against the goals you set together. Individual results vary, and we say so before you book.
